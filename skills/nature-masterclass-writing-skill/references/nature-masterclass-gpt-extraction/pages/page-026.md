**The Introduction**

Most people read at least some of the start of each paper they read, to understand the context for that paper – so the Introduction is important. But if you pick a few papers out of the literature at random, you’ll find that different papers take very different approaches. Some launch straight into highly detailed information that will leave non-specialists stranded, some include only a very broad discussion of the wider topic without much detail, and some papers barely include an Introduction section at all. Which approach is best?

**Aim and structure of the Introduction**

The most important aim of the Introduction is to set up the objective.

This means that starting with a bit of general background information on the overall topic can be important if you want your paper to be read by non-specialists, but this information should generally be brief. It should be directly relevant to your main argument – for example it should introduce any unusual concepts that underpin the paper (e.g. if you are studying a little-known phenomenon, it's usually a good idea to add a sentence or two about it, to explain what it is).

You should also help the reader understand why your objective is important (e.g. why knowing more about this phenomenon is useful, from a physics and/or applications perspective). That said, if your findings provide a fairly specialist insight into something that is widely known about, your target audience is unlikely to need any information on why this is an important topic.

Usually, much of the section should focus on the findings of studies that looked at similar objectives. What do we already know, both in the system you’re studying and any similar systems that might give us clues? And what do we not know that you aim to find out? In the abstract, you will normally have presented a ‘gap’ in our knowledge - here is your chance to delineate the edges of that gap, and show the reader why investigating your objectives is useful. For a methods paper, this will mean talking about the shortcomings in current approaches.

A golden rule is: **don’t forget to discuss the main papers that reach similar conclusions to you**. Otherwise, if you leave these papers until the Discussion (or not at all), readers will be frustrated to discover that something they thought was an original contribution of your paper was actually already known.

## Writing Principles

- The introduction is crucial because it contextualizes your paper for readers.
- The **objective** of the paper should be clearly set up in the introduction.
- Provide some general background but keep it concise, and only introduce details directly relevant to the argument or any unusual concepts critical to understanding your paper.
- Clearly articulate the **importance** of your objective; explain why investigating the topic matters.
- Focus on summarizing the findings of previous studies with similar objectives to show what is already known and what remains unknown (the 'gap').
- Define the boundaries of the knowledge gap and justify the significance of addressing it.
- For methods papers, organizationally highlight where current techniques or approaches fall short.
- **Explicitly reference major related works**—do not delay discussion of major overlapping literature until the Discussion section.

## Style / Structure Notes

- Use headings and sub-headings like “Aim and structure of the Introduction” to organize content.
- Start broad and general, then quickly narrow to specifics directly relevant to your paper.
- Arguments should progress logically: context → objective → significance → previous studies → knowledge gap.
- Important guidelines (like the golden rule about referencing similar work) are often set off in bold for emphasis.
- Avoid overloading the introduction with excessive background or technical details irrelevant to your main points.
