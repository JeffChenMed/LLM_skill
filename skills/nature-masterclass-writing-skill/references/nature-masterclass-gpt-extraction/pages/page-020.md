## Transcription

*(Venn Diagram illustration showing the relationships between the "Title," "Abstract," "Main Paper," and "Target audience")*

Most people find papers using internet searches or databases such as PubMed, in which only the title will be displayed in some views and only the title and abstract in others. An effective title should therefore encourage anyone who might find your research particularly useful and/or interesting to read the abstract, and an effective abstract should encourage some of these people to read the main paper. Once they have found out what they need to know, they will be in the position of being able to cite your paper and build on your research.

**Titles and abstracts are therefore extremely important long after publication.**

Most people will only read – and certainly only pay for access to – a paper that they are sure is relevant to them. Therefore, within reason, both the title and abstract need to be self-contained and accessible, so that potential readers can understand the findings.

## Writing Principles

- Titles and abstracts play a decisive role in whether a research paper is discovered and read; they serve as entry points for potential readers.
- An effective title should attract the interest of researchers who might benefit from or be interested in your work, motivating them to read further.
- An effective abstract should then encourage some of those readers to delve into the full paper.
- Both titles and abstracts should contain enough relevant information to allow readers to judge the usefulness of the main paper.
- Well-crafted titles and abstracts increase the chances that your work will be cited and built upon.
- Titles and abstracts remain important well after the initial publication, as they enable discoverability and relevance assessment in the long term.
- Both title and abstract need to be self-contained and accessible, written so readers—even those without access to the full paper—can understand the main findings.
