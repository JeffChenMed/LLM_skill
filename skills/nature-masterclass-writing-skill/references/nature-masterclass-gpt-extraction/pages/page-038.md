Editors at Nature Research journals select referees based on a number of factors, including:

- Their experience in the field
- Whether they have a broad overview of current trends and important issues (particularly important for review papers)
- Their efficiency (based on past referee experience)
- Whether they have a history of thorough and to-the-point reports or articles that are fair-minded and constructive
- No conflict of interest
- No positive or negative bias towards authors

The number of referees required is typically three, although this varies depending on the requirements of the paper. The use of multiple reviewers lessens the possibility of bias from an editor or a single referee.

Peer review can add significant value. Several different ongoing surveys by Nature Research journals place around 90% of scientists as feeling that their paper had been improved by the process at that journal.

**Being a referee**

If you are invited to review, you should first ensure that the manuscript falls within your area of expertise, that you actually have time to review it, and that you have no obvious conflicts of interest (e.g. you know the authors or you are working on the same question).

If you accept the invitation, the three key questions to ask are:

- **Do I understand it?** Are the methods and key research objectives clearly explained? Do the authors summarise and reference existing literature appropriately? Is it well presented?
- **Do I believe it?** Are the conclusions justified by the data and are the methods valid?
- **Do I care?** Is the main finding important and interesting? Is the work original?

## Writing Principles

- Referee selection should be based on expertise, broad knowledge, efficiency, fair-mindedness, and lack of conflicts or biases.
- Multiple reviewers are needed to reduce bias and add value through peer review.
- As a potential referee:
  - Only accept if the manuscript matches your expertise, you have time, and there are no conflicts of interest.
  - Evaluate the manuscript by asking yourself:
    - Is the work understandable, well-explained, and properly referenced?
    - Are the findings credible and methods valid?
    - Is the work important, interesting, and original?
