**The Results Section**

The results (or equivalent) are the defining feature of any paper that describes original research. And the Results section is where you present the core of your work and describe the most important features and patterns of your data. Because of this, rather than just being somewhere to put your results, **the Results section should be presented as a clear and succinct narrative.** Without this, readers might not understand why one set of findings led you to your next experiment, they might not see what you see in the dataset and they might therefore not reach the conclusions as you.

Here’s a very general template that can be useful in a variety of situations in the Results section:

> [Link to previous paragraph.] To [aim of the study], we [method].  
> We found that [key feature of results (fig/table ref)]. [Key feature of results (fig/table ref)]. [Key feature of results (fig/table ref)]. This suggests [conclusion from these results].

## Writing Principles

- The Results section is the central component of a research paper, where the key findings and data patterns are reported.
- More than a list or repository for results, the Results section should be crafted as a **clear and succinct narrative**.
- Good results writing helps readers:
    - Follow the logic of your experimentation.
    - Understand the significance of each result.
    - See how findings connect to subsequent experiments and overall conclusions.
- Use a template or structure that connects methods, findings, supporting evidence (figures/tables), and conclusions logically.

## Style / Structure Notes

- Headings (such as “The Results Section”) are clearly marked and set off in bold/red.
- Importance is placed on narrative cohesion—results should not be isolated points but presented in a logical, flowing manner.
- The suggested results paragraph template encourages authors to:
    - Link back to previous sections for coherence.
    - State aim and method succinctly.
    - Present findings clearly, referencing specific figures or tables.
    - Conclude each set of results with their implications or what they suggest.
- Visual elements (colorful bar diagrams labeled “EXPERIMENT 2” and “EXPERIMENT 3”) imply that results are often broken down by experiment or sub-section, each with its own narrative.
